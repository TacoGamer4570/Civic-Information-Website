how the simulator works

The building blocks

Each state is split into precincts (about 9,700 in Texas, 18,500 in California, and 9,100 in Pennsylvania). A district is a group of precincts. Each precinct carries three facts:

  • Population from the 2020 Census. Every census block is placed in the precinct that contains it.
  • Votes for Harris and Trump in 2024.
  • Neighbors: the precincts it shares a border with, and how long that border is. A few islands are linked to the nearest precinct across the water.

The algorithm (MCMC)

It starts from a random map made by cutting random spanning trees into equal-population pieces. It then proposes thousands of small changes, two kinds at random:

  • ReCom: merge two neighboring districts, draw a random spanning tree over their precincts, and cut it into two equal-population halves. Both halves are connected by construction.
  • Flip: move one precinct on a border into the next district. The move is only allowed if the district it leaves stays in one piece.

Each change gets a score. Better changes are always kept. Worse ones are sometimes kept, more often early on, so the chain can escape dead ends (this is the Metropolis rule, used as simulated annealing).

The three phases

  1. Balance population until every district is within your tolerance of the ideal size.
  2. Hit the partisan goal. The score measures how far the map is from the target seat split, counting a seat only if it's won by your safe margin. For "gerrymander" goals the target keeps rising until the chain can't reach the next seat.
  3. Make it compact. The seat result is locked in, and the chain minimizes total district "roundness" cost (Polsby–Popper: 1.0 is a circle, lower is more stretched), with a penalty for splitting counties if you asked for one.

Last, every district is checked with a graph search to confirm it's one connected piece.

What it can't do

  • Precincts aren't divisible, so population can't be balanced to the exact person the way real congressional maps are.
  • "Seats" here mean who carried the district in the 2024 presidential race. Real House races differ.
  • It doesn't model the Voting Rights Act, communities of interest, or incumbents.
  • Results vary run to run. That's the point of a random algorithm: try a few seeds.
