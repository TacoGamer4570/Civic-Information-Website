how the simulator works

The building blocks

The map is made of 2020 Census blocks, the smallest areas the Census counts: from about 32,000 in New Hampshire to 669,000 in Texas. Each block carries:

  • Population, the exact 2020 Census count.
  • Votes for Harris and Trump in 2024, estimated. Results are only reported by precinct, so each precinct's votes are split among its blocks by voting-age population.
  • Neighbors: the blocks it shares a border with, and how long that border is, straight from the Census's own map. Districts must be connected over land: a district can't hop across a bay, lake, or wide river. The only exception is land that water cuts off completely (islands, Michigan's Upper Peninsula, Virginia's Eastern Shore), which is linked across the water to any other land within 3 km (or, if there's none, to the nearest land). Water blocks follow the land next to them, and still count toward a district's shape. Sliver blocks (road and rail strips and other slivers under about 30 m wide on average) are too thin to see on the map, so they can't hold a district together: each one goes with the neighboring block it shares the most border with, and a district can't run along a sliver to connect two of its parts. A sliver only counts as a connection where it's the only land between two areas, such as a crossing over a river.

The algorithm (MCMC)

It starts from a random map made by repeatedly cutting random spanning trees in half. It then proposes huge numbers of small changes, two kinds at random:

  • ReCom: merge two neighboring districts, draw a random spanning tree over them, and cut it into two equal-population halves. Both halves are connected by construction.
  • Flip: move one piece of a border into the next district. The move is only allowed if the district it leaves stays in one piece.

Each change gets a score. Better changes are always kept. Worse ones are sometimes kept, more often early on, so the chain can escape dead ends (this is the Metropolis rule, used as simulated annealing).

The phases

  1. Balance population until every district is within your tolerance of the ideal size. For the tightest settings it also trades a block one way and a slightly smaller block back in a single step, so the net change can be a few people even where every border block holds hundreds. If the finished map still has a district outside the tolerance, that map is usually a dead end, so it's thrown away and the run starts over from a new random map with a new seed (the results say how many times). Skip phase or stopping ends it early and keeps the closest map.
  2. Hit the partisan goal. The score measures how far the map is from the target seat split, counting a seat only if it's won by your safe margin. For "gerrymander" goals the safe-seat margin is a floor: the chain first packs in as many districts as possible won by at least that much. With that number locked, it maximizes wins under the floor. Then it works through every other district one at a time, best first: it pushes that district as close to the floor as it will go, locks it in, and moves to the next, never giving up a seat it already has. Safe seats aren't frozen: they can give up surplus votes to help the districts below, as long as they stay at or above the floor. For "proportional", the goal is the seats–votes curve itself, not an exact seat count: the chain bends the curve toward a straight 1-for-1 line, so that each party's share of seats matches its share of the vote, and if the whole state swung a point toward either party, that party would win about one more point of seats, and the same for a 5- or 10-point swing. That means spreading the districts' vote shares evenly, one step of 1/(number of seats) apart, centered on the statewide vote. A map that hits the line usually lands on the proportional seat split too, but it isn't forced to: a map closer to the line wins over one with an exact split. You choose how the line is scored. Weighted around the 2024 vote (the default): swings of up to 10 points from the 2024 result count most, up to 25 points half as much, bigger ones a little, since the far ends of the line call for districts at 10% or 90%, which most states can't draw. Evenly within ±20 margin points (national-adjusted): the line is centered on what the state would vote in a tied national election (its 2024 margin shifted by the national margin, R+1.5, so a D+10 state is centered on D+11.5), and every statewide margin within 20 points of that center counts the same (for that D+10 state, from R+8.5 to D+31.5). Nothing outside that range counts. Whole line equally: every statewide vote share from 0% to 100% counts the same, so a lopsided state is also judged on what a 50/50 vote would do.
  3. Make it compact (unless you chose to ignore compactness). The seat result is locked in (for proportional maps, the seats–votes curve is: it can't drift more than a quarter point from where it was), and the chain minimizes total district "roundness" cost (Polsby–Popper: 1.0 is a circle, lower is more stretched), with a penalty for splitting counties if you asked for one.
  4. Refine with census blocks. For speed, steps 1–3 move whole precincts (built from their blocks). Then the map switches to individual blocks: it balances population much more tightly and smooths the borders, with the seat result still locked. With very many districts, every step runs on blocks.

Last, every district is checked with a graph search: on each land mass it must be a single piece connected over land, so no part of a district can be stranded inside another one and reached only across water. The same rule is enforced on every move along the way.

Counties: "Required"

With Keep counties whole: Required the map splits as few counties as it can, then only edits districts with whole counties:

  1. Whole counties. Each county is one unit (counties with more people than a district have to be split anyway, so those use their precincts). The goal is chased here.
  2. Split as few as possible. Whole counties rarely add up to equal districts, so the districts are linked in a tree (preferring pairs that already share a split county). Along each link, people move first inside a county the two districts already share, then by moving whole counties, and only if that isn't enough by splitting one county. That's at most one new split per link, and often none.
  3. Whole-county edits. Every unsplit county is now a single unit that can only move as a whole. Split counties work by precinct, so the lines inside them can still shift. The goal and compactness are refined, and the number of split counties can only go down from here.
  4. Census blocks. Population is evened out exactly and borders smoothed, but only inside counties that are already split. Small leftover pieces of a county are put back with the rest of it where they can be.

A county is always treated as connected to itself here, even where a road or rail strip is all that joins two parts of it. If population can't be evened out without one more split, it allows one more and says so in the results.

Changing settings without starting over

Once a map is drawn, you can change any setting (goal, safe-seat floor, shape, population tolerance, counties, effort, superthinking) and press Apply to this map. The new run starts from the districts on screen instead of a random map, so you can, say, draw a fair map and then gerrymander it. Start over always draws a fresh random map. Changing the number of districts or the state needs a fresh map.

Superthinking

With Superthinking on, the whole sequence runs again and again. Each finished cycle is compared with the best map so far: first on your goal (seats won, how close to your target split, or for proportional maps, how close to the 1-for-1 seats–votes line), then on compactness (or population balance, if you're ignoring compactness). Only maps within your population tolerance and fully connected count. Most cycles start from the best map and push further, with gerrymander goals always aiming one seat higher, and later cycles search harder. Every fourth cycle starts from a fresh random map to get out of dead ends. It never stops on its own, even if your goal can't be reached; press Stop & keep best.

What it can't do

  • Block-level votes are estimates. Two blocks in the same precinct are assumed to vote alike.
  • "Seats" here mean who carried the district in the 2024 presidential race. Real House races differ.
  • It doesn't model the Voting Rights Act, communities of interest, or incumbents.
  • Results vary run to run. That's the point of a random algorithm: try a few seeds.
