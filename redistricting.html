how the simulator works

The building blocks

The map is made of 2020 Census blocks, the smallest areas the Census counts: from about 57,000 in Nevada to 669,000 in Texas. Each block carries:

  • Population, the exact 2020 Census count.
  • Votes for Harris and Trump in 2024, estimated. Results are only reported by precinct, so each precinct's votes are split among its blocks by voting-age population.
  • Neighbors: the blocks it shares a border with, and how long that border is, straight from the Census's own map. Districts must be connected over land: a district can't hop across a bay, lake, or wide river. The only exception is land that water cuts off completely (islands, Michigan's Upper Peninsula, Virginia's Eastern Shore), which is linked across the water to any other land within 3 km (or, if there's none, to the nearest land). Water blocks follow the land next to them, and still count toward a district's shape.

The algorithm (MCMC)

It starts from a random map made by repeatedly cutting random spanning trees in half. It then proposes huge numbers of small changes, two kinds at random:

  • ReCom: merge two neighboring districts, draw a random spanning tree over them, and cut it into two equal-population halves. Both halves are connected by construction.
  • Flip: move one piece of a border into the next district. The move is only allowed if the district it leaves stays in one piece.

Each change gets a score. Better changes are always kept. Worse ones are sometimes kept, more often early on, so the chain can escape dead ends (this is the Metropolis rule, used as simulated annealing).

The phases

  1. Balance population until every district is within your tolerance of the ideal size.
  2. Hit the partisan goal. The score measures how far the map is from the target seat split, counting a seat only if it's won by your safe margin. For "gerrymander" goals the safe-seat margin is a floor: the chain first packs in as many districts as possible won by at least that much. With that number locked, it maximizes wins under the floor. Then it works through every other district one at a time, best first: it pushes that district as close to the floor as it will go, locks it in, and moves to the next, never giving up a seat it already has. Safe seats aren't frozen: they can give up surplus votes to help the districts below, as long as they stay at or above the floor.
  3. Make it compact (unless you chose to ignore compactness). The seat result is locked in, and the chain minimizes total district "roundness" cost (Polsby–Popper: 1.0 is a circle, lower is more stretched), with a penalty for splitting counties if you asked for one.
  4. Refine with census blocks. For speed, steps 1–3 move whole precincts (built from their blocks). Then the map switches to individual blocks: it balances population much more tightly and smooths the borders, with the seat result still locked. With very many districts, every step runs on blocks.

Last, every district is checked with a graph search to confirm it's one connected set of blocks.

Superthinking

With Superthinking on, the whole sequence runs again and again. Each finished cycle is compared with the best map so far: first on your goal (seats won, or how close to your target split), then on compactness (or population balance, if you're ignoring compactness). Only maps within your population tolerance and fully connected count. Most cycles start from the best map and push further, with gerrymander goals always aiming one seat higher, and later cycles search harder. Every fourth cycle starts from a fresh random map to get out of dead ends. It never stops on its own, even if your goal can't be reached; press Stop & keep best.

What it can't do

  • Block-level votes are estimates. Two blocks in the same precinct are assumed to vote alike.
  • "Seats" here mean who carried the district in the 2024 presidential race. Real House races differ.
  • It doesn't model the Voting Rights Act, communities of interest, or incumbents.
  • Results vary run to run. That's the point of a random algorithm: try a few seeds.
